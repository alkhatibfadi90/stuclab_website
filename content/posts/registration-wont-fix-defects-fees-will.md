Every time an apartment building in Australia cracks badly enough to make the news, the profession's answer is the same. Register the engineers. Opal Tower, Mascot Towers, Neo200, and each time a push for a registration scheme, first state by state and now a joint submission for a national one. Most of the engineers I know support it, and so do I, for what it does. It just doesn't do the thing people think it does.

![Stacked bars comparing a sustainable fee with a fee cut by half: drawings unchanged, modelling trimmed, checking and senior review gone; a timeline showing both sets look identical at issue and the difference appears at year three; the inquiry asks about registration and then about hours](/posts/fee-vs-hours.svg)

## What registration certifies

Registration certifies a person. It says this engineer has a recognised degree, enough years, references, and has agreed to a code of conduct. That is worth having, and it's baffling that it took a string of evacuations to get it.

What it does not certify is the hours. It says nothing about whether the engineer who signed the drawings had the time to check them, whether the coordinator ran the model or a graduate did, or whether the transfer level got one review or three. Defects come from those hours, not from the credential of the person who didn't get them.

## Where the hours went

Structural engineering fees on a typical building sit somewhere around one to two percent of construction cost, and that figure has been falling for a generation. Firms routinely report being undercut by half on jobs they'd already priced thin. Developers run three engineers against each other to shave a fraction of a percent off a budget that will spend more on the lobby finishes.

Think about what a fee at half the sustainable rate actually buys. The same drawings, because the drawings are the deliverable and the client can see them. Fewer checking hours, because checking is invisible. A more junior team, because that's the only lever left. The output looks identical on the day it's issued. The difference shows up in year three, on a Saturday, with residents in the carpark.

## The engineer who wins on fee is the one in the inquiry

This is the uncomfortable part. The fee brawl is not something done to the profession; it's something the profession does to itself. Every firm that takes a job at a number it knows can't fund proper review is making the same bet: that the building will be fine, and it usually is. The bet pays out until it doesn't, and when it doesn't, the inquiry will look at the credential on the certificate, find it in order, and then ask how many hours were spent. Registration will have answered the first question and been silent on the second.

## What would actually move the number

Registration gives engineers something they didn't have before: legal standing as a named profession in building legislation. That is the lever. It makes it possible to argue, with the regulator, that a structural engineer's scope has a minimum, and that a fee which can't fund that minimum is a compliance problem, not a commercial one. Other professions did this decades ago. Ours is only now getting the standing to try.

Until then, the only control is the one each firm already has: the bid you don't submit. A fee that can't pay for the review is not a competitive price. It's a liability you've agreed to carry for free.
