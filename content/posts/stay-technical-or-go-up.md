Somewhere between year six and year ten, every structural engineer gets asked a version of the same question. Sometimes it's a manager asking whether you'd like to run a team. Sometimes it's a job ad with "lead" in the title and a salary band you haven't seen before. Sometimes nobody asks, and you just notice that the people who started with you are now sitting in different meetings.

The question is always framed as a choice between two tracks. Technical or management. Specialist or leader. Both treated as equally real, equally open, equally respected. Most of that framing is wrong, and knowing which parts are wrong is what lets you choose with your eyes open.

## The technical track is real in some firms and a slogan in others

Every consultancy says it has a technical career path. Few can show you one. The test is simple: name the people who are two levels above you on it, what they're paid relative to a group manager, and who decides their promotion. If the answers are vague, the track is a retention slogan, not a path. It exists to stop good engineers leaving when they turn down the team lead role, and it quietly tops out one or two steps later.

Where the technical track is real, it looks like this: a title with Principal or Technical Director in it, a pay band that overlaps with management, a defined role in bids and reviews, and a seat in the room where the big calls are made. That exists. It is rarer than the brochure suggests, and it is almost never available in the office you happen to be sitting in. If you want it, you may have to move to get it.

## Management is a different job, not a promotion

The second thing nobody says plainly: running a team is not a reward for being good at engineering. It is a separate profession that your firm will hand you with no training, on the assumption that the skills carry across. They don't. Resourcing, fee negotiation, performance conversations, client politics, cash flow on a job that's gone bad; none of it was in the degree and none of it comes from being the best modeller on the floor.

Some people find they're good at it and like it. Many find they're adequate at it and miss the work. The honest way to test which you are is to do a piece of it before you commit to all of it: run one job end to end, own the fee and the client, carry one junior. Six months of that tells you more than any amount of thinking about it.

## The decision is made by default long before it's made on purpose

Here's the part that actually matters. Most engineers never choose. The firm needs a team lead, the senior person is available, the pay is better, and the decision is made for them by timing. Five years later they're competent managers with a technical reputation that has quietly expired, and the way back is far harder than the way up.

That asymmetry is the whole game. Going up and then coming back to pure technical work is possible but rare, because your technical currency stops accruing the day you stop doing the work. Staying technical and going up later is easier, because management skills can be picked up at forty in a way that modelling instincts cannot. So if you're unsure, the conservative move is to stay technical a little longer than feels comfortable, not a little shorter.

## The question to actually ask

Forget the two-track diagram. Ask instead: what do I want to be the person people call for, at forty-five? If the answer is "the hardest structural problem in the building," you need depth, and you need to protect the hours that build it even when the firm would rather spend them elsewhere. If the answer is "the person who makes the whole job work," you need breadth, and you should take the team role now and take it seriously.

Both answers are good. The only bad answer is the one you didn't give, because then the firm's resourcing spreadsheet gives it for you.
