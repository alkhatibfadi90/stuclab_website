The message is already landing in inboxes. A data vendor working with the labs that build the big models wants to license real engineering work: calculation packages, drawing sets, specifications, submittals, analysis models. Not synthetic examples. Your jobs. They'll pay, and the pitch is that the models need to learn how a project is actually put together. Engineers on the forums are asking each other whether to take the money.

Before you reply, three things are worth being clear about, because the offer is designed so that you don't stop to think about any of them.

![Five layers of a calc package, from arithmetic at the bottom to review markups at the top, with ownership per layer and a value arrow rising toward the judgement layers; an employee's personal share is close to nothing](/posts/calc-package-layers.svg)

## You almost certainly don't own what they're asking for

Look at the terms on any consultancy agreement you've worked under. The deliverables are usually assigned or licensed to the client. The working files, the models, the spreadsheets, belong to the firm. The methodology might be the firm's, might be the client's, depending on who drafted the contract. What belongs to you personally, as an employee, is close to nothing. Every one of those documents also sits under a confidentiality clause that was written for exactly this: handing a third party the structural design of a building you were paid to keep private.

So the question is not whether you'd like to sell. It's whether you have anything to sell. If you're a sole practitioner with your own templates and your own anonymised examples, maybe. If you're thinking of the job files on the firm's server, no, and the firm would be within its rights to treat it as a breach.

## What they're buying is not the numbers

The vendor isn't paying for a beam calc. The arithmetic is already in the textbooks. What they want is the judgement wrapped around it: which load case you ran and which you dismissed, how you handled the transfer that didn't fit the template, the note that says why the modifier is 0.35 and not 0.7, the markup where a senior crossed something out. That is the part of the work that takes fifteen years to learn and that no amount of code text teaches a model. It is also the part that currently justifies your rate.

You'd be paid once, at a licence fee set by a buyer who knows the value better than you do, for something that reduces the scarcity of what you sell every day after.

## The honest version of the trade

None of this means engineers should keep their work locked away forever. The profession would benefit from models that have seen real practice. The honest version of that looks different from the message in your inbox: the profession or the firm deciding what to contribute, on terms it controls; anonymised and client-cleared; priced as an ongoing licence rather than a one-off; and with some say in what the resulting tool does and who gets it. That conversation is worth having at the Engineers Australia or Consult Australia level. It is not one to settle by replying to a LinkedIn DM at 9pm.

## What to actually do

Don't reply. Forward it to whoever handles contracts at the firm and let them answer it. If you're independent, ask three questions before anything else: who owns this, what does the confidentiality clause say, and what would I charge if I knew this was the last time anyone would pay me for it. If any of the three makes you uncomfortable, you have your answer.

The work in those files is the most valuable thing you've produced in your career. Treat an offer to buy it the way you'd treat an offer to buy anything else you spent fifteen years building.

*General commentary, not legal advice. IP and confidentiality terms vary by contract; check yours before sharing anything.*
